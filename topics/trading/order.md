Setup:

    Your Budget: $1,000

    Current Stock Price (close_price): $100

    Broker Commission: $10 (flat fee)

Correct Calculation (using close_price)

    Estimate Quantity: You can afford roughly $1000 / $100 = 10 shares.

    Calculate Total Cost: (10 shares × $100/share) + $10 commission = $1,010.

    Adjust Quantity: Since $1,010 is over your budget, you must buy fewer shares. Let's try 9.

    Final Cost: (9 shares × $100/share) + $10 commission = $910. This is within your budget.

    Your order quantity is 9.

# Fields `signal` and `order_type`

`signal` and `order_type` fields should **not** always be the same. keeping them as separate columns is a best practice that makes your system more flexible and your data easier to analyze.

while they are closely related, they represent two different stages of the trading process: the **strategic intent** and the **execution instruction**.

***
### the core difference: "why" vs. "how"

think of the two fields as answering different questions about your trade.

* **`signal` (the "why")**: this field represents the **strategic decision** generated by your trading algorithm. it's the high-level intent. for example, your strategy decided to `buy` because the 10-day moving average crossed above the 50-day moving average.
* **`order_type` (the "how")**: this field represents the **specific execution instruction** you send to the broker to act on your signal. it determines *how* the trade will be filled in the market. 

### scenarios where `signal` and `order_type` differ

here are a few common examples where the signal to buy or sell is not a simple market order.

* **placing a limit order**: your signal is `buy`, but you don't want to buy at the current market price. you want to buy only if the price drops to a specific level.
    * `signal`: "buy"
    * `order_type`: "limit"
* **setting a stop-loss**: your signal is `sell`, but it's a defensive order to protect a position. you only want to sell if the price falls to your stop-loss level.
    * `signal`: "sell" (or a more specific signal like "stop_loss")
    * `order_type`: "stop" or "stop-market"
* **using a trailing stop**: your signal is `sell`, but you want the sell order to trail the market price by a certain percentage to lock in profits on a winning trade.
    * `signal`: "sell" (or "trail")
    * `order_type`: "trailing_stop"

### conclusion

keeping `signal` and `order_type` as separate columns is the correct design. it provides greater clarity by separating your strategy's intent from the execution details, gives your system the flexibility to support different types of orders, and creates a richer dataset for analyzing the performance of your trading logic in the future.


# Order Types

A **limit order** is significantly safer to begin with for your first automated trading activities.

it gives your code absolute control over the price, which is the most critical factor to manage when you're starting out.

***
## why a limit order is safer

a limit order protects you from one of the biggest risks in automated trading: **slippage**.

* **price control**: a limit order guarantees that you will not pay more than your specified price on a buy, or receive less than your specified price on a sell. your order will only execute at your limit price *or better*. this makes your costs predictable and prevents your algorithm from buying at a price much higher than intended during a sudden spike.
* **protection against volatility**: if the market suddenly becomes volatile between the time your code decides to trade and the time the order reaches the exchange, a limit order protects you. a market order, in the same situation, could be filled at a terrible price.

the only downside to a limit order is the risk that it might not be filled if the market price never reaches your limit. however, for a beginner, **it's far safer to miss a trade than to enter one at a disastrous price**.

## the risk of market orders

a **market order** guarantees execution but provides zero price protection. it will be filled at the best available price at the moment it hits the exchange.

while this is useful for getting in or out of a position quickly, it's risky for a new automated system because you can't be 100% sure what the fill price will be. in a "flash crash" or a highly volatile market, your market order could be executed far from the `close_price` your code initially saw, leading to an immediate, significant loss.

## practical example

imagine your code sees a stock's last price at **$100.00** and decides to buy.

* **you send a limit order** to buy at `$100.05`. the best you'll pay is `$100.05`. if the price has already shot up to `$101.00` by the time your order arrives, your order simply won't be filled, protecting you from buying at the high.
* **you send a market order**. in the split second it takes for your order to travel, the price jumps to `$101.00`. your order will be filled at `$101.00`, instantly putting you at a loss compared to your intended entry point.

**conclusion**: start with **limit orders**. they enforce a critical layer of safety by ensuring your automated system behaves predictably and never overpays. once you're confident in your system's logic and pre-trade checks, you can consider using market orders in specific, highly liquid situations.

this strategy is often called adding a **price buffer**, an **offset**, or **aggressiveness factor**. it intelligently balances the safety of a limit order with a higher probability of getting your order filled.

***
## why it's a good idea

a pure limit order placed exactly at the last `close_price` or `bid_price` might never get filled if the price ticks up even slightly after your decision. by adding a small buffer, you increase your chances of a quick execution while still protecting yourself from major slippage.

it essentially tells the market: "i want to buy at this price, but i'm willing to pay a tiny bit more to ensure i get the shares."



## what's the optimal rate to begin?

there's no single "optimal" rate, as it depends heavily on the **volatility** and **bid-ask spread** of the stock you're trading.

* **highly liquid stocks** (like apple or microsoft) have very tight spreads, so a smaller buffer is effective.
* **less liquid or more volatile stocks** have wider spreads, so they might require a larger buffer to get filled.

a starting rate of **0.05% to 0.10%** is a very reasonable and conservative place to begin.

* **0.05%** on a $100 stock is only a 5-cent difference ($100.05).
* **0.10%** on a $100 stock is a 10-cent difference ($100.10).

**my recommendation**: start with a conservative rate like **0.05%**. the most important step is to **backtest** your strategy with different buffer percentages to see what works best for the specific assets you're trading.

## example in your code

here's how you could implement this logic in your python code.

```python

limit_price_buffer_percent = 0.0005 # this is 0.05%

# --- your trading logic ---
last_close_price = current_ticker_data.close

# calculate the slightly higher limit price

# let's format it to a standard price format (e.g., 2 decimal places)
